Teaching & Mentoring

Make analogies between a complex topic and easily understood phenomena; ask questions that scaffold students from the familiar to the complex; guide students to apply the newly understood topic creatively to real-world problems.

One of my greatest satisfactions as a first-generation research scholar is explaining complex concepts in ways that spark understanding, curiosity and excitement for science. I have led study groups and taught since school, served as a teaching assistant and tutor in chemistry, physics and mathematics as an undergraduate, and taught classes and workshops in materials science and computational and physical chemistry during my doctoral studies. I always integrate my research into my teaching, and my teaching back into my research.

How I put this into practice

Active engagement through easily understood phenomena

I want students to hold a bird's-eye view of fundamental concepts and to see how those concepts generate new knowledge. Supervising eight undergraduates in UBC's Research Experience (REX) program, I used clickers and live polling to visualize ideas and gather real-time feedback, and introduced simulation and machine learning in small, accessible steps — what data is, how pattern recognition drives discovery, how those principles appear in computational chemistry — before advancing to regression and classification. Students left asking how ML applies beyond chemistry; three later won Mentee of the Year awards at a multidisciplinary research conference. I pair this with a flipped classroom, distributing lecture notes in advance so class time concentrates on what students found hardest.

Promoting belonging

I aim for an inclusive, equitable learning environment in which the diversity students bring is treated as a strength rather than accommodated as an exception: implicit-bias training for the whole teaching team, universal design applied to materials and assessment, and anonymous mid-course feedback acted on while the students who gave it are still in the room. This is grounded in practice — I have tutored more than 150 undergraduates in chemistry, chemical engineering and physics, mentored three PhD students and two postdoctoral fellows, guided more than 30 students in CHEM 250 (Inorganic Chemistry for Chemical Engineers), and provided free chemistry tutoring to Black students in the UBC Mastercard Foundation program.

Creative application to real-world problems

In every course I stress the breadth of real-world challenges addressable with straightforward chemical engineering and applied chemistry principles. Volunteering with SciFro Inc. in Ethiopia, I organized sessions teaching more than 40 African students to address local health problems through inexpensive experiments — a talk on fluorescence and microscopy followed by a hands-on demonstration with a cardboard fluorescence viewer and a Foldscope costing under USD 30. Students experienced science directly, and the curiosity it generated is exactly what I want in an engineering classroom.

Supportive mentoring

I tailor my approach to each trainee's goals, background and needs while holding high academic standards that encourage independent thought. As a professor I will meet mentees biweekly to set expectations and give constructive feedback, and build an annual Individual Development Plan with each of them. Recognizing that access to research is unequal, I will ensure every group member is supported through a paid position or academic credit, and create peer-mentoring structures and student-led projects in self-driving labs and materials chemistry. I measure my success by my trainees' growth.
